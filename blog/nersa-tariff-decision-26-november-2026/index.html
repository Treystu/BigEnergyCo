NERSA decides on 26 November: solar and the fixed charge

Last checked: 14 November 2026 · Published: 14 November 2026 · Lucas Ballek

The percentage everyone quotes was decided in February. On 26 November 2026 the Energy Regulator rules on something narrower and, for a household with panels, more expensive: how Eskom recovers that revenue — through the energy rate, or through the fixed service and capacity charges that no amount of self-consumption can touch. This post is about that decision and the two implementation dates behind it, not about the headline increase.

What is actually being decided on 26 November

NERSA's consultation paper on Eskom's 2027/28 retail tariff structural adjustment (ERTSA) is explicit that the process does not reopen the allowable revenue: the Energy Regulator approved the revised revenue — and with it the average increase — on 7 February 2026, after the High Court set aside the 2026/27 and 2027/28 determination and remitted it. The application filed on 31 July 2026 asks the Regulator to approve the Schedule of Standard Prices and Charges that recovers it.

Inside that schedule sits the part that decides a solar home's payback: the third year of the Retail Tariff Plan, which continues the Generation Capacity Charge and proposes adjustments to the Homepower and Homeflex fixed service charges, with the energy rate trimmed to hold the average at the approved figure.

Why the structure matters more than the percentage

A rooftop system reduces the energy line. It does not reduce the fixed service charge, the network capacity charge or the generation capacity charge — those are billed for being connected and for the capacity you are supplied, not for the units you use. So when a regulator shifts recovery from energy into those lines, the same roof saves less, and the payback that a solar quote shows stretches out without anything about the hardware changing.

That is the whole reason this decision deserves its own post rather than a line in a tariff round-up: it is a decision about the mix, and the mix is what a solar calculation is sensitive to.

A worked example, with every assumption on the table

Assumptions, labelled as assumptions: a bill of R3,000 a month, of which R700 is fixed charges and R2,300 is energy at an implied rate; a system that cuts the energy line by 70%. These are example numbers, not your bill and not a tariff schedule.

Monthly bill before and after a 70% energy cut
Line Before After
Fixed charges R700 R700
Energy R2,300 R690
Total R3,000 R1,390

The saving is R1,610 a month — 54% — even though the panels cut the energy line by 70%. The gap between those two numbers is the fixed share. Every increase in that share reduces the best case a solar system can reach, regardless of how many panels go on the roof.

Run your own bill through the calculator rather than this example: it asks for your fixed charge and your rate separately, and sizes the system against the part that can actually move.

What to do before 26 November

  • Find your tariff name. Homepower 1–4 and Homeflex tiers are billed differently; a municipal account is on a different schedule entirely, with its own date (1 July 2027).
  • Add up the fixed lines on your actual bill. Service and administration, network capacity, generation capacity — if you cannot find them separately, that is itself the finding: an unbundled bill is what makes this decision legible.
  • Compute the ceiling. Fixed charges divided by total bill is the share solar can never touch. If it is a third of your bill, no system takes you below two-thirds.
  • Do not sign on the pre-decision numbers. A quote priced against today's split will overstate the saving if the approved schedule moves more cost into the fixed lines.

What this post does not claim

The rand amounts of the proposed fixed charges are not in the consultation paper — they sit in Eskom's application annexures — so this post does not repeat them as fact, and it does not predict the Regulator's decision. What the paper does say, in its own words, is that the application continues the Generation Capacity Charge and proposes adjustments to the Homepower and Homeflex fixed service charges. That direction is enough to change how a solar quote should be read.

Frequently asked

Is the 8.83% decided on 26 November?

No. The average increase follows from the allowable revenue approved on 7 February 2026 by the Energy Regulator after the High Court remitted the 2026/27 and 2027/28 determination. The 26 November decision is on the schedule of standard prices and charges that implements it.

When do the new numbers reach my bill?

1 April 2027 if Eskom supplies you directly; 1 July 2027 if you buy from a municipality, because municipal tariffs follow the MFMA calendar.

Does a battery help with fixed charges?

No — nothing on the fixed lines moves with consumption. A battery helps with the energy line and with time-of-use pricing, and both are worth sizing properly in the calculator.

Frequently asked

Is the 8.83% increase confirmed?

The 8.83% overall path was set in NERSA's MYPD6 redetermination of 7 February 2026. The 26 November 2026 decision is on the tariff structure and price schedule that recovers it, which is where the fixed-versus-energy split is set. Check NERSA's published decision after that date rather than relying on a headline.

Will solar reduce my fixed monthly charge?

No. A fixed service or network charge is billed for the connection. Solar displaces the per-kWh energy line, and a battery shifts when you buy from the grid.

Does a bigger system always save more?

Not in rand. Once your daytime load is covered, extra generation is exported at the tariff's credit rather than avoiding the full energy rate, so the marginal saving per extra kW falls. Size against the energy you displace.

What changes for municipal customers?

Municipal tariffs normally follow on 1 July rather than 1 April, and municipalities set their own charge structures within NERSA's approval. Read your own municipality's schedule; the Eskom split does not transfer automatically.
