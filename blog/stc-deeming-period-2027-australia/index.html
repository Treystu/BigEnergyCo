STC Deeming Period Falls to 4 Years on 1 Jan 2027

Last checked: 11 November 2026 · Published: 11 November 2026 · Lucas Ballek

From 1 January 2027 a rooftop solar system installed in Australia is deemed to generate certificates for four years instead of five, because the Small-scale Renewable Energy Scheme runs to 31 December 2030 and the deeming period is the years of the scheme that remain. The panels, the inverter and the installer's price do not change. The rebate does, and it falls by a fifth. This page does the arithmetic rather than the deadline theatre: what the step-down costs, and what chasing it could cost you.

The schedule, from the regulator's own table

2025 6 years

2028 3 years
2029 2 years

This is a scheduled annual reduction, not a policy surprise: the scheme lapses at the end of 2030 and the certificate count shrinks with it.

How certificates are counted

The regulator's formula for a solar PV system is:

STCs = system kW × postcode zone rating × deeming years, rounded down

The zone rating is a sunshine factor attached to your postcode, not to your nearest city, and the regulator publishes it as a table rather than a rule of thumb. The four ratings are 1.622 (zone 1), 1.536 (zone 2), 1.382 (zone 3) and 1.185 (zone 4). Sydney, Brisbane, Perth, Adelaide and Canberra sit in zone 3 and Melbourne and Hobart in zone 4, but a postcode that is close to a boundary is exactly where people get this wrong — look yours up.

What the step-down costs, in dollars

A 6.6 kW system is the common Australian residential size, so use it. Certificates are counted at the clearing-house price of $40 each, a fixed number the regulator administers; the open-market spot price moves around it (it sat near $39.62 in the regulator's Q1 2026 report), so a quote that assumes a higher price is making an assumption you can check.

6.6 kW system, certificates and value at $40
Zone Rating 2026 (5 yr) 2027 (4 yr) Loss
Zone 1 1.622 53 STCs · $2,120 42 STCs · $1,680 $440
Zone 2 1.536 50 STCs · $2,000 40 STCs · $1,600 $400
Zone 3 1.382 45 STCs · $1,800 36 STCs · $1,440 $360
Zone 4 1.185 39 STCs · $1,560 31 STCs · $1,240 $320

So the honest headline for most of the population is about $360 less on a 6.6 kW system, not thousands. The percentage is bigger than the number: nine fewer certificates is a fifth of the rebate.

The part the deadline posts leave out

"Install before January to lock in the higher rebate" is true as far as it goes, and still usually bad advice on its own. Rushing a purchase to capture $360 has three costs that are easy to ignore:

  • You buy a year of electricity early. A system installed in December instead of March generates power you would otherwise have bought for three months — which may be more than the rebate difference, or less, depending on your tariff.
  • Quotes get worse in a rush. The discount is a fixed number of certificates; a rushed install can easily absorb the difference in a padded price, and you will not see it on the quote.
  • Prices are falling. Modules and batteries have been getting cheaper, so a January install is buying hardware in a cheaper market than a December one. Nobody can promise which effect wins for you — that is what the sizing arithmetic is for.

The decision is a comparison, not a countdown: rebate difference versus months of generation versus quote quality.

Batteries step down too, every six months

A battery is counted under a separate factor that changes twice a year. For an eligible battery of 5 to 100 kWh nominal capacity, the factor is 6.8 certificates per kWh from 1 May to 31 December 2026 and 5.7 per kWh from 1 January to 30 June 2027. That is a steeper relative fall than the solar step-down, and it applies to the battery alone — a battery quoted as "free" or "heavily discounted" is usually showing you this factor, not a price cut.

What to check before you sign anything

  • Your postcode's zone rating, from the regulator's table — not from the state capital.
  • Which deeming year your quote assumes. The date that counts is the installation date on the certificate of compliance, not the date you signed.
  • The certificate price in the quote. Ask for it explicitly; the clearing-house price is $40 and the spot price moves.
  • The 100 kW cap. Residential systems are nowhere near it, but a farm or a small business quote may be.

Price the system on your own consumption, not on the rebate. Enter your location and usage: the calculator sizes the array from hourly climate data and shows the levelised cost beside what you pay the grid.

Frequently asked

Is the 8.83% increase confirmed?

The 8.83% overall path was set in NERSA's MYPD6 redetermination of 7 February 2026. The 26 November 2026 decision is on the tariff structure and price schedule that recovers it, which is where the fixed-versus-energy split is set. Check NERSA's published decision after that date rather than relying on a headline.

Will solar reduce my fixed monthly charge?

No. A fixed service or network charge is billed for the connection. Solar displaces the per-kWh energy line, and a battery shifts when you buy from the grid.

Does a bigger system always save more?

Not in rand. Once your daytime load is covered, extra generation is exported at the tariff's credit rather than avoiding the full energy rate, so the marginal saving per extra kW falls. Size against the energy you displace.

What changes for municipal customers?

Municipal tariffs normally follow on 1 July rather than 1 April, and municipalities set their own charge structures within NERSA's approval. Read your own municipality's schedule; the Eskom split does not transfer automatically.
