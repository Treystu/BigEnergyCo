Eskom's Fixed Charge and Solar: 2027 Payback Math

Last reviewed: November 11, 2026 · Published: November 11, 2026 · Lucas Ballek

On 26 November 2026 NERSA is due to decide Eskom's retail tariff structure for 2027/28 — the schedule that splits the regulator's already-approved revenue between a fixed monthly line and a per-kWh line. The headline number people quote is 8.83%. For a household with solar, the number that matters is smaller than that, because part of the increase is recovered from a charge you pay whether or not your panels are producing. This guide works out what that does to the rand you actually save.

What is being decided, and when

Two things are easy to confuse, and the difference decides how much your solar is worth:

  • The revenue is already approved. NERSA's MYPD6 redetermination of 7 February 2026 set an 8.83% overall Eskom price path for 2027/28.
  • The structure is not. The November decision is on Eskom's Retail Tariff and Structural Adjustment (ERTSA) application — the price schedule that recovers that revenue, and therefore the split between fixed service charges and energy rates. Eskom's own FY2028 application states that the fixed service charges on the Homepower and Homeflex residential tariffs step up to 100% of the Retail Tariff Plan rand-value in this third year (Eskom FY2028 ERTSA application, Annexure B).

Implementation is 1 April 2027 for Eskom direct customers, with municipal tariffs normally following on 1 July. The 2026/27 year shows the same mechanism already running: the fixed portion of the service and administration charge rose to 66.66% of the originally proposed FY2025 rand-value, up from 33.33%, with energy rates lowered to keep the average increase at 8.76% for residential customers (Eskom 2026/27 tariff page; the 2026/27 decision is NERSA's ERTSA decision and reasons).

What a solar household can and cannot avoid

A fixed monthly service charge is not avoidable by generating your own electricity. It is billed for being connected, and the proposed schedule raises it. What your system avoids is the energy line: every kWh you serve yourself is a kWh you do not buy at the per-kWh rate.

Because the two lines move in opposite directions to hold the average at 8.83%, a heavier fixed line means a lighter energy line. That makes each self-consumed kWh worth slightly less than it was, even though your total bill would still rise without solar. This is the part that most savings claims leave out.

A worked example in rand

Take a household buying 900 kWh a month on a Homepower-type tariff. Figures are illustrative and rounded; the point is the arithmetic, not the exact cents, and the real numbers are on your own bill.

Same household, before and after a heavier fixed line
Reading Energy rate R2.90, fixed R600 Energy rate R2.67, fixed R750
Bill with no solar 900 × 2.90 + 600 = R3,210 900 × 2.67 + 750 = R3,153
Bill with solar covering 80% 180 × 2.90 + 600 = R1,122 180 × 2.67 + 750 = R1,231
Rand saved per month R2,088 R1,922
Percent of the bill cut 65% 61%

The percentage bill cut falls as well as the rand: the fixed line is a larger share of the bill, and solar cannot touch it. Two consequences follow, and both are design decisions rather than slogans:

  • Self-consumption beats export. A kWh you use behind the meter avoids the full energy rate; a kWh you export is worth whatever the tariff credits, which is a different and usually lower number.
  • Oversizing for a percent target is the wrong aim. Past the point where your daytime load is covered, extra panels improve the percentage and barely improve the rand. Size against the energy line you can actually displace.

Where a battery changes the arithmetic

A battery moves solar energy from the middle of the day to the evening, where it displaces energy you would otherwise buy. The arithmetic has three parts:

  • What you shift. If you shift 8 kWh a day, that is about 240 kWh a month of grid energy you no longer buy.
  • What the round trip costs. At about 90% round-trip efficiency, delivering 240 kWh takes about 267 kWh of solar, so about 27 kWh is spent on losses. If those kWh would otherwise have been exported for a small credit, that is the real cost of shifting.
  • What the battery costs over 20 years. Capex, plus any replacement, divided by the kWh it actually delivers — the calculator's own levelized figure does this division for you.

At R2.67 a kWh, 240 kWh a month is about R641 of avoided energy, before the loss cost above. Whether that clears the battery's own cost per kWh is the whole question, and it is a calculation, not a sales claim. This guide deliberately does not print a hardware price: landed-DIY and turnkey quotes in South Africa differ by more than the subsidy debates do, so use a written quote and the calculator's levelized row.

What to do before 26 November

  • Read your own split. Find the fixed service charge and the energy rate on your latest bill. The ratio tells you how much of your bill solar can reach.
  • Size against energy, not percent. Enter your kWh and your country in the calculator; the bill-cut target is a slider, and the honest answer is the system that displaces the energy you actually buy.
  • Re-check after the decision. The 26 November outcome sets the split. If fixed charges rise further than proposed, the rand savings per self-consumed kWh fall again.

Size the system against the line you can displace. Enter your location and monthly use; the calculator prices the system from local hourly weather and shows the levelized cost per kWh beside the grid rate.

Frequently asked

Is the 8.83% increase confirmed?

The 8.83% overall path was set in NERSA's MYPD6 redetermination of 7 February 2026. The 26 November 2026 decision is on the tariff structure and price schedule that recovers it, which is where the fixed-versus-energy split is set. Check NERSA's published decision after that date rather than relying on a headline.

Will solar reduce my fixed monthly charge?

No. A fixed service or network charge is billed for the connection. Solar displaces the per-kWh energy line, and a battery shifts when you buy from the grid.

Does a bigger system always save more?

Not in rand. Once your daytime load is covered, extra generation is exported at the tariff's credit rather than avoiding the full energy rate, so the marginal saving per extra kW falls. Size against the energy you displace.

What changes for municipal customers?

Municipal tariffs normally follow on 1 July rather than 1 April, and municipalities set their own charge structures within NERSA's approval. Read your own municipality's schedule; the Eskom split does not transfer automatically.
